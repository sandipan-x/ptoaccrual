How PTO Accrual Actually Works

Many employers do not hand you vacation on January 1st — you earn it gradually, a little with every paycheck. A salaried employee granted 15 days a year on a biweekly schedule accrues 15 ÷ 26 = 0.58 days per pay period. An hourly employee might earn 0.0577 PTO hours for every hour worked — 4.6 hours per 80-hour pay period. Either way, the balance ticks upward continuously, which is why checking it quarterly (not annually) is the habit that saves people from nasty surprises.

The accrual model exists to protect employers from new hires taking three weeks in February and quitting in March. But it creates its own traps for employees: caps that silently pause your accrual, use-it-or-lose-it cliffs, and payout rules that vary wildly by state. This guide covers the mechanics, the traps, and the simple system for never donating vacation back to your employer.

Doing the Per-Paycheck Math

For salaried policies, the formula is annual grant ÷ pay periods per year. Weekly pay (52 periods) accrues the smallest slices; monthly pay (12) the largest. Fifteen days a year means 0.29 days weekly, 0.58 biweekly, 0.63 semimonthly, or 1.25 monthly. These look tiny per check — that is the point; they compound into real vacations.

For hourly policies, multiply the rate by hours worked: 0.0577 × 80 hours = 4.62 hours per biweekly period, or about 15 days a year for full-time work. Part-timers accrue proportionally less, which surprises people switching from full-time: your rate did not change, your hours did. Note the common rate landmarks: 0.0385 ≈ 10 days/year, 0.0577 ≈ 15 days/year, 0.0769 ≈ 20 days/year for full-time schedules.

Carryover Caps: The Silent Balance Killer

A carryover cap limits how much PTO you can hold — commonly 1 to 1.5× your annual grant (so 20–22.5 days on a 15-day grant). The cruel mechanic: accrual pauses while you are at the cap. Every paycheck at the cap is vacation time you earned on paper and will never receive. Unlike a use-it-or-lose-it cliff, there is no dramatic deadline — just a slow leak of a day here, half a day there, invisible on most pay stubs.

The calculator flags exactly when your projection hits the cap and how many days you would forfeit. The defense is scheduling: project your balance a quarter ahead (the timeline above does this visually) and book time off before the cap bites. If your balance hovers near the cap year-round, that is data — you are structurally unable to use what you earn, and it is worth raising with your manager, not just accepting.

Use-It-or-Lose-It and State Law

Use-it-or-lose-it policies wipe unused PTO at year-end (or at an anniversary date). They are legal in most states if the policy is written and communicated — but California, Colorado, Illinois, and Montana ban forfeiture of earned vacation outright. In those states, "use it or lose it" is unenforceable, and accrued vacation is treated as wages you own.

Payout at termination follows similar lines. In California, Colorado, Illinois, and Montana, earned unused vacation must be paid out when you leave — it is wages. In most other states (Texas, New York, Florida, and others), payout is required only if company policy promises it — check your handbook, because the promise is often there in writing. A subtle distinction in several states: vacation may be payable while sick leave is not, so know which bucket your PTO sits in. None of this is legal advice — but it tells you which questions to ask HR before you assume.

Tenure Bumps and Front-Loading

Many companies increase grants with tenure — 10 days for years 1–2, 15 for years 3–5, 20 beyond. If a bump lands mid-projection, model the year in two segments rather than averaging; the calculator's "periods ahead" field handles any horizon. Some employers front-load (grant the full year upfront) instead of accruing — simpler for employees, riskier for employers. A few do unlimited PTO, which studies consistently show leads to employees taking less time off; if that is your policy, set your own annual target (15+ days) and treat it as a quota.

The Quarterly System That Prevents Losses

Here is the entire system: every quarter, project your balance to year-end using the calculator above, and if the projection exceeds (cap − planned time off), book a trip. That is it. Four two-minute checks a year eliminate both cap forfeiture and December panic-booking. Put a recurring calendar reminder for the first Monday of each quarter — "PTO check" — and keep the calculator bookmarked.

Two more habits. First, request early for peak periods — summer weeks and holidays are first-come, first-served in most teams, and "I couldn't get the time approved" is how balances die at the cap. Second, track in days, think in experiences: "7.77 days" is abstract; "a full week in October plus two long weekends" is a plan you will actually execute.

Negotiating PTO (Yes, It's Negotiable)

Vacation is one of the most negotiable — and least negotiated — parts of a job offer. Companies with rigid salary bands often have flexible PTO; asking for 20 days instead of 15 costs them little and buys you a lot. Frame it as tenure recognition ("my current role gives me 18 days") rather than a demand. And get it in writing: verbal PTO promises evaporate, while handbook language endures. If they will not move on days, negotiate the accrual start (immediate vs 90-day wait) or remote-work flexibility as consolation.

Sick Leave vs. Vacation: Know Your Buckets

Not all PTO is the same bucket, and the distinction has legal teeth. Vacation is generally treated as earned wages in payout states — accrued, bankable, and payable at termination in California, Colorado, Illinois, and Montana. Sick leave is often treated differently: many states that mandate sick-leave payout exemptions let employers expire it, cap it lower, or deny payout entirely. When your employer offers a single combined "PTO" bucket, the whole balance usually inherits vacation's stronger protections; when they split them, each bucket plays by its own rules.

This matters in three practical moments. First, at hiring: ask whether the buckets are combined or separate, because 15 days of combined PTO is worth more than 10 vacation + 5 sick days that cannot be repurposed. Second, at the cap: some employers cap vacation but not sick leave, or vice versa — the calculator's cap field should reflect the bucket you are actually modeling. Third, at exit: in payout states, your vacation balance converts to a final paycheck line item, while sick leave typically evaporates — so in your last months, spend sick days on actual illness and preserve vacation for the payout. None of this is legal advice, but knowing which bucket you are looking at changes every decision the calculator informs.

Frequently asked questions

How does PTO accrual work?

You earn vacation gradually each pay period — e.g., 4 hours per biweekly paycheck or 0.0577 hours per hour worked. Your balance grows until you use time off or hit a cap.

How much PTO do you earn per pay period?

Annual grant ÷ pay periods: 15 days/year biweekly = 0.58 days per check. Hourly plans commonly use 0.0385–0.0769 PTO hours per hour worked.

What is a PTO carryover cap?

A limit on banked PTO, often 1–1.5× your annual grant. At the cap, accrual pauses — every paycheck at the cap is earned time you never receive.

Is use-it-or-lose-it PTO legal?

In most states, yes, if the policy is written. But California, Colorado, Illinois and Montana prohibit forfeiting earned vacation. Check your state law and handbook.

Do companies have to pay out unused PTO when you quit?

In CA, CO, IL and MT, yes — it is wages. Elsewhere, usually only if company policy promises it. Vacation and sick leave are often treated differently.

How do I avoid losing PTO to a cap?

Project your balance quarterly, schedule time off before hitting the cap, and set a calendar reminder — accrual pauses at the cap, so idle balances cost you real days.
